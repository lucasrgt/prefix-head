\begin{abstract}
In small language models with large subword vocabularies, the output head is a large share of the per-token cost: 21--63\% of the weight bytes read per generated token in Gemma~3 270M, Qwen3-0.6B and Llama~3.2~1B. A two-level (class-factored) softmax reduces output-weight reads by approximately $\sqrt{V}/2$-fold, and has returned in 2025--26 in speculative-decoding drafters and drop-in head replacements; what remains open is \emph{how to form the classes}. We show that the simplest possible rule---sort the subword vocabulary by spelling and cut it into equal blocks---gives the most faithful factored head among the standard grouping baselines in four of the five models tested. Swapping the head of five frozen open models (Gemma~3 270M/1B, Gemma~4 E2B, Qwen3-0.6B, gpt-oss-20b; three vendors, four tokenizers), among frequency, semantic, random and token-id baselines, prefix-ordered classes have the lowest KL divergence to the original model in four of five (semantic $k$-means has a slightly lower mean on the fifth), and 27--33\% lower KL than the token ordering Google ships with its Gemma~4 drafters, at equal cost, including on Gemma~4 itself. Trained from scratch, prefix classes outperform the tested alternative groupings in recurrent and MLP models, with replication in English and a gap that shrinks as the vocabulary grows. A word-start/frequency and continuation/prefix hybrid further improves fidelity in four models at square-root-sized blocks. A Transformer with a prefix head is within 0.45\% of the full head in bits per character at 4.2$\times$ fewer total MACs. A per-decision and word-start/continuation decomposition explains why: the first decision follows the generation direction, and the gain concentrates on tokens that continue a word---which also explains why alphabetical classes lost at the whole-word level (Jiang et al., 2017). On a CPU at batch size one, the factored head raises Gemma~3 270M throughput by 2.4$\times$ in end-to-end, one-token-at-a-time scoring (2.1$\times$ with AVX2 only).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The output projection of a language model maps a hidden state $h\in\mathbb{R}^d$ to logits over a vocabulary of size $V$. Vocabularies have grown (128k in Llama~3, 152k in Qwen3, 201k in gpt-oss, 262k in Gemma) while the models people run locally have stayed small, so in small models the head is no longer a rounding error: it is 63\% of the bytes read per token in Gemma~3 270M (Table~\ref{tab:share}). At batch size one---a model on a laptop or phone, or a draft model in speculative decoding---generation is memory-bound, and those bytes are time and energy.

Class-factored softmax \citep{goodman2001classes,mikolov2011extensions} is the classical remedy: predict a class, then a token inside the class, each with a small softmax; with $C\approx\sqrt{V}$ classes of $m\approx\sqrt{V}$ tokens the head reads $(C+m)d$ instead of $Vd$ weights. It fell out of use on GPUs with large batches, where the full head is cheap, and because it costs quality. It is back: Gemma~4's drafters score 2{,}048 token clusters and expand the top 32 \citep{gemma4}; FlashHead replaces heads with balanced spherical $k$-means clusters and multi-probe retrieval \citep{flashhead}; CORAL factors a drafter head into equal groups \citep{coral}; FR-Spec and VocabTrim restrict drafter heads by frequency \citep{frspec,vocabtrim}. All of these group tokens by embedding similarity, frequency, or token id.

We ask which grouping makes the factored head most faithful, at equal cost, and find that a rule needing no data and no model---\emph{sort the vocabulary by spelling, cut into equal blocks}---outperforms standard grouping baselines in four models. Section~\ref{sec:variations} studies further improvements using a word-start/continuation hybrid. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Retrofit evidence on five frozen open models} (Section~\ref{sec:retrofit}): among the standard grouping baselines, prefix classes give the lowest KL to the original head in four of five models (semantic classes have a slightly lower mean in the fifth), and beat the ordering shipped with Gemma~4 by 27--33\% in KL at equal cost.
\item \textbf{From-scratch evidence} across MLP, recurrent and Transformer models, two languages and vocabularies of 8k--100k (Section~\ref{sec:scratch}), including the honest finding that the gap shrinks with vocabulary size.
\item \textbf{A mechanism} (Section~\ref{sec:why}): the class decision becomes easy because it follows the generation direction; the gain is 4--6$\times$ larger on word-continuation tokens. This reconciles our result with the opposite whole-word finding of \citet{jiang2017}.
\item \textbf{End-to-end CPU throughput and head-only GPU cost} at batch one (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\item \textbf{Variations} (Section~\ref{sec:variations}): a word-start/continuation hybrid, controls that isolate the mechanism, three-level trees, block size, and a failed refinement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Two-level head.} Given a partition of the vocabulary into classes $c(t)$ with members $M_c$, the head computes
\[
\log p(t\mid h) = \log \mathrm{softmax}(A h + b)_{c(t)} + \log \mathrm{softmax}\big(\ell_{M_{c(t)}}(h)\big)_{t},
\]
where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}$ predicts the class and $\ell_{M_c}(h)$ are the original logits restricted to that class (including logit soft-capping where used). This defines a normalized distribution over the entire vocabulary: no token is excluded. It reproduces the full head only when the predicted class probabilities equal its class marginals. Sampling draws a class, then a token. At inference the head reads $C\cdot d$ class weights and one block of $m\cdot d$ token weights.

\paragraph{Groupings compared.} \emph{Prefix}: sort tokens by their byte spelling, cut into equal blocks of $m$. \emph{Frequency}: sort by training frequency (the criterion of adaptive softmax and frequency-trimmed drafters). \emph{Token id / merge order}: the tokenizer's own order (CORAL-style groups). \emph{Random}. \emph{Semantic}: balanced $k$-means on the output embeddings (as in FlashHead and, by our inspection, Gemma~4). \emph{Gemma~4}: the \texttt{token\_ordering} buffer released with the Gemma~4 E4B drafter, cut into its 2{,}048 blocks of 128.

\paragraph{Retrofitting a frozen model.} We keep the model and its output rows frozen and train only $A,b$. In the \emph{self} setting the class predictor is trained to reproduce the model's own class marginals $p(c\mid h)=\sum_{t\in M_c}p_{\text{full}}(t\mid h)$ on training text (no labels, no other model), so its deviation from the full head is the pure cost of factoring. We report $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{full}}\,\|\,p_{\text{2-level}})$ in bits per character on held-out text; because $p(t\mid c,h)$ is exact, this equals the class-level KL. In the \emph{adapt} setting both the full head and the two-level head (class predictor and blocks) are fine-tuned on the same text with the same budget. The self-trained predictor uses Adam for two epochs, batch size 512, initial learning rate $10^{-3}$ with linear decay, and class-centroid initialization; the output rows in the adapted heads use learning rate $10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:kl}. Among the standard grouping baselines, prefix is the most faithful grouping in four of five models; on Gemma~3 1B semantic $k$-means is marginally better (0.0854 vs.\ 0.0867, within one s.d.\ of its seed spread), but semantic classes are worse than frequency on Qwen3 and Gemma~4. At equal cost prefix blocks deviate 27--33\% less than Gemma~4's own ordering, including on Gemma~4.

\paragraph{Bits per character, and a calibration caveat.} On base models the factored head costs a little: Gemma~3 1B-pt 1.561$\to$1.615 bpc (+3.5\%; 1.600 with 128-blocks), Qwen3-0.6B 1.918$\to$1.942 (+1.2\%), gpt-oss-20b 2.685$\to$2.697 (+0.5\%). We therefore use KL to the original model, not bpc, as the primary retrofit metric.

\paragraph{Equal-budget adaptation.} Fine-tuning both heads on the same text (Gemma~3 270M, Qwen3-0.6B): full 1.794 / 1.612 bpc; prefix 1.819 / 1.661 (+1.3\% / +3.0\%); semantic 1.842 / 1.751; frequency 1.896 / 1.724; random 1.872 / 1.723; token id 1.916 / 1.759. With 128-blocks on Gemma~3 270M: prefix +0.8\%, Gemma~4 ordering +3.0\%.

Two hypotheses could explain the effect. \emph{Separability}---semantic classes force a hard choice between synonyms inside a class---is refuted: the within-class decision is \emph{easier} for semantic classes (0.69 vs.\ 0.88 bits/char overall). \emph{Direction} is supported: with prefix classes the first decision asks how the next piece of text begins, which the context predicts well, especially when a word is in progress. Sorting by the second character instead gives an intermediate result. Table~\ref{tab:split} localizes the gain: 4--6$\times$ larger on continuation tokens, and almost entirely in the class decision (1.28 vs.\ 1.54--1.95). With whole-word vocabularies every token starts a word, where the effect is small---consistent with \citet{jiang2017}, where alphabetical classes lost to frequency and Brown clusters.

\paragraph{With 4-bit weights.} Google's quantization-aware-trained release of Gemma~3 270M in Q4\_0 keeps the tied $262\text{k}\times640$ embedding/head in F16: 335\,MB of a 399\,MB file. Our runtime reads Q4\_0 and F16 natively. With the full F16 head, the head takes 71\% of each token's time (98 tokens/s, AVX-512, 8 threads); with the prefix head (512 classes of 512, class predictor trained on the QAT model's own states) throughput rises to 430 tokens/s (4.4$\times$; run-to-run range 381--483), 3.4$\times$ the Q8\_0 model with its full head (126 tokens/s in the same session). KL to the QAT model's own head is 0.120 bits/char (0.100 with 128-token blocks), comparable to the Q8 model. Weight quantization and head factorization compose. Requantizing the F16 head to 8 bits (absmax per 32 weights) costs nothing measurable (2.423 vs.\ 2.421 bits/char with the prefix head on 512 tokens) and shrinks the model from 393 to 235\,MB; with it the prefix head reaches 537 tokens/s (5.5$\times$ the shipped QAT model, 4.3$\times$ the Q8\_0 model with its full head). A 4-bit head saves a further 83\,MB but costs 5\% in bits/char. For reference, llama.cpp (commit a11f57b, 8 threads, full heads) generates 129 tokens/s with the QAT Q4\_0 model and 175 tokens/s with the Q8\_0 model on the same machine; our runtime is about 25--30\% slower than llama.cpp with the full head, so the prefix-head figures are not explained by a faster body. Naively requantizing the non-QAT Q8 model to 4 bits instead costs 10\% in bpc for the body alone, which is why we use the QAT release.

\paragraph{A 1.58-bit model.} BitNet b1.58 2B4T \citep{bitnet2025} is trained with ternary weights, yet its tied $128\text{k}\times2560$ embedding/head is stored in F16: 657\,MB against 523\,MB for the whole ternary body in the released GGUF, 56\% of the bytes read per token. We extracted its final hidden states with the reference implementation (online ternarization, \texttt{tools/gpu/hidden\_hf.py}) and swapped the head as in Section~\ref{sec:retrofit} (358 classes). KL to the full head: prefix $0.1218\pm0.0001$ (3 seeds), frequency 0.125, token id 0.224, semantic $0.206\pm0.007$, random 0.264, and the word-start/continuation hybrid 0.110; bits per character 2.169 (full) $\to$ 2.193 (prefix). Reading only the class matrix and one block cuts per-token reads from about 1.18\,GB to 0.53\,GB, an upper bound of about $2.2\times$ for memory-bound decoding; our runtime does not implement the BitNet architecture, so this is not measured end to end.

\section{Variations}\label{sec:variations}

All numbers in this section are KL to the original model (bits/char, self setting, Section~\ref{sec:retrofit}); prefix and hybrid rows are means over 3 seeds (s.d.\ $\le$0.0003), the rest single runs (\texttt{tools/gpu/explore.py}, \texttt{results/explore.jsonl}).

\paragraph{Mixing criteria and controls.} Table~\ref{tab:variants}. Ordering word-start tokens by frequency and word-continuation tokens by spelling (\emph{hybrid}) improves on pure prefix in four models (by 2--11\%) and is 0.6\% worse on Gemma~4, at $\sqrt V$-sized blocks. With 128-token blocks the hybrid is worse than pure prefix on all three Gemmas (e.g.\ Gemma~3 1B: 0.0886 vs.\ 0.0724), though still better than the Gemma~4 ordering (0.1086). Three controls support the mechanism: ordering word starts semantically instead of by frequency is worse; ignoring the leading space, which merges word-start and continuation spellings, is worse; and sorting by the \emph{reversed} spelling (suffix order) is far worse---similar spelling is not what matters, the beginning of the next piece is. Conversely, sorting under a randomly permuted byte alphabet (three permutations), which keeps every prefix family together but changes which families are neighbours, matches ordinary prefix order (Qwen3: 0.088--0.096 vs.\ 0.096; Gemma~3 1B: 0.086--0.089 vs.\ 0.087): the prefix tree matters, not the alphabet.

\paragraph{Depth.} A three-level prefix tree (super-class, class, token; e.g.\ $64\times64\times64$ for 262k tokens) reads 808--1{,}365$\times$ fewer output rows than the full head. Its KL is 1.3--1.8$\times$ that of the two-level prefix head (Gemma~3 1B: 0.124 vs.\ 0.087; Qwen3: 0.127 vs.\ 0.096), and lower than that of the same three-level structure over a random order in every model (Gemma~4 E2B: 0.262 vs.\ 0.493; gpt-oss: 0.036 vs.\ 0.056). The relative advantage over random order increases with depth on the Gemmas and Qwen3, but decreases on gpt-oss-20b; its dependence on depth is therefore model-specific. Because the two-level head already takes only 1--2\% of a token's time on CPU (Section~\ref{sec:cost}), deeper heads matter mainly for drafters whose body is tiny next to a 262k vocabulary.

\paragraph{Granularity.} Prefix blocks have lower KL than random blocks at every block size with a matched random control. Prefix-only sweeps cover block sizes from 64 to 4{,}096 (up to 1{,}024 for gpt-oss-20b); random controls are available at 128, approximately $\sqrt V$, and 2{,}048 (the first two for gpt-oss-20b). The shape of the cost--fidelity curve is model dependent: for the Gemmas and Qwen3, many small classes beat few large ones in the tested configurations (Qwen3 at a 116$\times$ vs.\ 72$\times$ cut: 0.075 with 128-token blocks vs.\ 0.101 with 2{,}048-token blocks), whereas gpt-oss-20b becomes \emph{less} faithful with small blocks (0.037 at 64 vs.\ 0.020 at 449).

\paragraph{Speculative-decoding acceptance.} Gemma~3 270M as drafter, Gemma~3 1B and Gemma~4 E2B as targets (identical tokenizations, 214k positions). We report the per-position acceptance of a sampled draft token, $\alpha=\sum_t\min(p_t,q_t)$, a teacher-forced proxy for speculative decoding \citep{leviathan2023spec}. With the exact two-level head and 128-token blocks (2{,}176 rows read), prefix blocks keep $\alpha=0.477$ / $0.371$ (targets 1B / E2B) against $0.460$ / $0.345$ for the Gemma~4 ordering and $0.490$ / $0.396$ for the full drafter head: prefix loses about half as much acceptance. Gemma~4's own scheme---score the classes, keep the top 32, softmax over their tokens (6{,}144 rows)---is nearly lossless with any ordering ($0.453$--$0.488$ / $0.397$--$0.398$), and with it the Gemma~4 ordering is better than prefix on the 1B target ($0.480$ vs.\ $0.453$); under that scheme greedy acceptance is the same for every ordering ($0.447$ / $0.384$). So for drafters verified by a target, top-$k$ screening makes the ordering nearly irrelevant, and prefix ordering does not improve it; the exact prefix head reaches similar acceptance on the 1B target while reading 2.8$\times$ fewer rows, and remains the option for standalone models that need probabilities for every token.

\paragraph{Exact culling.} We also tried to find the exact argmax token while reading fewer rows, with bounding spheres per block (centre plus radius, as in ray-tracing BVHs) and with a low-precision copy of the head whose per-row error bound decides which rows to recompute. At $d=640$--$1024$ both bounds are too loose: on Gemma~3 270M and Qwen3 essentially every block is visited and almost every row refined, for prefix, random and semantic blocks alike.

\paragraph{Refinement.} Starting from prefix blocks, we moved each token to the class whose trained predictor already assigns high probability in that token's contexts (capacity-limited) and retrained, for three rounds. Every round was worse than the prefix start in every model (e.g.\ Qwen3: 0.096 $\to$ 0.167, 0.132, 0.145), so this heuristic does not improve on spelling order; whether a direct KL-optimizing search can is left open.

\section{Related work}

Class-based and hierarchical softmax \citep{goodman2001classes,morin2005hierarchical,mikolov2011extensions}; adaptive softmax \citep{grave2017adaptive}; tree-based hierarchical softmax with alphabetical, Huffman and Brown trees at the word level \citep{jiang2017}; prefix-tree lexicons for speech decoding search \citep{ney1992}. Screening and approximate heads: SVD-softmax \citep{shim2017svd}, L2S \citep{chen2019l2s}, FlashHead \citep{flashhead}. Drafter heads: Gemma~4 \citep{gemma4}, CORAL \citep{coral}, FR-Spec \citep{frspec}, VocabTrim \citep{vocabtrim}. Training-memory reduction of large heads without changing inference cost: Cut Cross-Entropy \citep{wijmans2025cut}. To our knowledge no prior work orders a subword vocabulary by spelling to form output classes.

\section{A frugal recurrent body (secondary study)}\label{sec:quartzo}

For CPU deployment we pair the head with a diagonal complex linear recurrence $h_t=(1-z_t)\,e^{i\theta}h_{t-1}+z_t c_t$ with input-only gates and a learned, data-independent rotation. We arrived at it independently, but it is the HGRU recurrence of HGRN \citep{qin2023hgrn}; our differences are a chrono-style log-uniform initialization of gate biases and stateful truncated-BPTT training. At tiny scale (2.2M parameters, 2 layers, 51 complex channels per layer) it matches a 2-layer Transformer with the same head (2.063 vs.\ 2.063 bpc) at 3.6$\times$ fewer MACs per character and an 816-byte inference state instead of a growing 128\,KB KV cache, and beats our Mamba-2-style baseline (2.078 bpc at 1.6$\times$ the MACs). Removing the rotation ($\theta=0$, so the real and imaginary halves become $2\times51$ independent real channels in the same 816 bytes) costs 0.8\%: 2.079 bpc (2 seeds: 2.0799, 2.0789) against 2.063 (3 seeds: 2.0614, 2.0665, 2.0608). At this scale the rotation helps. Letting part of the channels update only every $k$ positions and hold their state in between (Clockwork-style; their read-out is then constant and can be cached) trades a little quality for fewer MACs: over 3 seeds, 80 channels with 50 updating every 8 positions cost +0.26\% bpc for 21\% fewer MACs per character, and 51 channels with 26 slow ones +0.46\% for 33\% fewer. Spreading the periods over powers of two (1 to 64, one seed) did not improve this trade-off. We also tried a synthetic associative-recall task (2--32 key--value pairs per line, 2M characters, 30 epochs): neither the recurrent core with or without rotation nor a same-width Transformer learned it within that budget (recurrent: 3--5\% accuracy on recalled characters, chance 3.8\%; Transformer: 5--7\%, declining with the number of pairs), so this probe is inconclusive and the known recall weakness of fixed-size states \citep{arora2023zoology,jelassi2024repeat} remains untested here.

\section{Limitations}

One text domain (19th-century public-domain literature) for all retrofit and most scratch results, plus English for replication. Retrofit models up to 21B parameters (3.6B active); scratch models are tiny. We evaluate likelihood, KL and a teacher-forced acceptance proxy, not downstream tasks or wall-clock speculative decoding. The factored head does not reduce the stored model size, only the bytes read per token. Gains at large batch on GPUs are modest.
